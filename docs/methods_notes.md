# Methods notes

Analysis choices, relevant references, and validation checks. Numbers come from `results/` and can be regenerated with
`run_all.sh` and `scripts/calibrate_tests.py`.

## Data

**Simulated.** 110 sessions, one Purkinje cell each, an overtrained block then
a block with a new symbol pair. Everything about these cells is invented by
`pcvm/simulate.py`; the ground truth is stored with each session. Task timing
follows the published task (fixation 800 ms, reward at movement onset, 700 ms
to the next trial). 94 sessions pass QC.

**Real.** `data/public/CS_DATASET.mat` from github.com/naveen-7/Cerebellum_reward,
the repository cited in the data statement of Sendhilnathan, Ipata & Goldberg
2021. 25 cells with trial-by-trial symbol, hand and outcome, and 1 ms rasters
aligned to symbol and movement. I use 22: one has six learning trials, two sit
at 16-17% correct across the learning block, which should not happen in a
two-choice task and which I cannot explain.

Two things to keep in mind about that file. It is named for the complex-spike
paper, but the events come at 3-122 per second (median 31), which is
simple-spike territory; I treat them as simple spikes. And nine of the 22 learning blocks
reuse the symbols of that session's first block with the mapping reversed, so
they are reversals, not new pairs.

A larger set (Mendeley Data, doi 10.17632/22n9ps5rzv.1, CC BY 4.0, "Purkinje
neurons recorded from two monkeys performing a visuomotor association learning
task") exists. It is not used here.

The simulation was tuned against the real file where it could be:

| | public cells | simulation |
|---|---|---|
| accuracy by thirds of the learning block | .54 / .77 / .94 | .55 / .74 / .91 |
| win-stay / lose-shift | .81 / .60 | .80 / .60 |
| across-trial CV of firing rate (beyond Poisson) | 0.34 (IQR 0.32-0.57) | drawn from 0.15-0.5 |
| lag-1 autocorrelation of counts across trials | 0.62 (IQR 0.46-0.72) | AR(1) with 0.6-0.95 |

The drift numbers are from `scripts/public_drift.py` (1.4 s of pre-symbol
activity per trial). They are an upper bound on pure drift, since some of that
variance could be task related.

## The question that needed the most care

The central published result is that simple spikes during learning carry the
outcome of the previous trial, in a short epoch whose timing differs between
cells, and that this fades as the association is learned. Testing that on one
cell means comparing trials that followed an error with trials that followed a
reward. There are three ways for that comparison to mislead.

1. **Searching over time.** The epoch is not known in advance, so every window
   in the trial gets tested. "Significant in a few consecutive windows" is not
   corrected for the search, and neighbouring sliding windows share most of
   their spikes, so one chance crossing brings its neighbours with it.
2. **Trials are not exchangeable.** Errors happen early in the block and
   rewards late. Firing rates drift (table above). Any slow change in rate is
   therefore correlated with "previous trial was an error" whether or not the
   cell knows about outcomes.
3. **Selecting and measuring on the same trials.** The window and the preferred
   outcome are chosen because the difference is large there, then the size of
   the difference in that window is plotted across learning.

### Relevant methodological work

- Harris 2020, "Nonsense correlations in neuroscience" (bioRxiv
  2020.11.29.402719). Compares ten ways to test association between two slowly
  varying series. Naive tests, circular shifts, phase randomisation and
  cross-validation all produce false positives. Two methods are sound:
  *pseudosessions* (regenerate the behavioural variable from the distribution
  the experimenter used) and *session permutation* (pair neural data with
  behaviour from other sessions). A conservative *linear shift* test is valid
  when one series is stationary. He is explicit that pseudosessions cannot be
  used for variables that depend on the animal, and recommends session
  permutation there.
- Elber-Dorozko & Loewenstein 2018 (eLife 7:e34248). The same problem in the
  setting closest to mine, learning plus trial-by-trial regressors. About 40%
  of random-walk "neurons" were classified as action-value neurons by the
  standard regression; permuting within blocks and adding lagged spike counts
  did not fix it; comparing each neuron with regressors from other sessions did.
- Findling et al. 2025 (Nature 645:192, the IBL prior paper). Uses 1,000
  pseudosessions for a variable generated by the task. For the variable that
  depends on the animal's own choices they state that a null cannot be built,
  because it would need the exact statistical model of behaviour, and they
  test a task-generated substitute instead.
- Nevjen & Dunn 2024 (eLife reviewed preprint 92517). Cyclic shifts plus
  cross-validation for tuning to continuous covariates; suggests checking
  false-positive rates by swapping covariates between experiments.
- Nichols & Holmes 2002 (Hum Brain Mapp 15:1) for the maximum statistic over
  a search space; Kriegeskorte et al. 2009 (Nat Neurosci 12:535) on selecting
  and measuring on the same data; Walther et al. 2016 (NeuroImage 137:188) on
  cross-validated distance estimates that are zero on average when there is
  no difference.

### Choosing a previous-outcome test

The outcome of a trial is not generated by the experimenter. It is the
animal's choice checked against a random stimulus. So the method with the
cleanest guarantee (pseudosessions) does not apply, and I had to choose among
methods that each lean on something:

| null | what it assumes | cost |
|---|---|---|
| session permutation | sessions are interchangeable replicates | needs many sessions; p-values no finer than 1/(n sessions) |
| synthetic sessions from a learning model fitted to this session | the model reproduces the time structure of this animal's outcomes | the assumption IBL declined to make |
| trend covariates + shuffle | drift is a smooth function of trial number | fails on jumps |
| linear shift | one of the two series is stationary | neither is, during learning; low power |
| local permutation: shuffle outcomes only within runs of 8 neighbouring trials, with one intercept per run | outcomes are exchangeable within 8 trials | throws away between-run information |

All of them use the same statistic, the largest absolute partial correlation
between sqrt spike count and the regressor over 200 ms windows stepped by 50
ms, so each gives one p-value per cell with the search over time already paid
for. The implementation is `tools/outcome_tests.py`.

I also added a control that the 2020 paper used in a different form (their
"1+N" comparison): test the outcome of the **next** trial. Nothing can respond
to feedback it has not received, so a method that finds next-outcome cells is
finding drift, or a slow state such as engagement that changes both error rate
and firing.

### Calibration on simulated cells

1,000 simulated sessions per condition, 60-200 trials, learner deliberately
different from the model used for synthetic sessions. Percent of cells
significant at .05 (`results/calibration_table.md`, figure
`docs/figures/calibration.png`):

| | no drift | drift as in the real cells | slow drift | rate follows learning state | rate jump + step-like learner | real signal (power) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| per-window t, 3 in a row | 13.5 | 24.9 | 41.3 | 37.0 | 57.7 | 65 |
| t, Bonferroni | 3.9 | 9.1 | 18.7 | 19.2 | 34.5 | 45 |
| shuffle | 4.3 | 12.6 | 23.5 | 24.4 | 41.6 | 52 |
| circular shift | 4.8 | 6.0 | 11.0 | 14.2 | 14.0 | 44 |
| linear shift | 2.7 | 2.2 | 2.0 | 1.3 | 2.3 | 26 |
| session permutation + trend | 3.9 | 3.4 | 5.7 | 4.1 | 4.6 | 52 |
| synthetic sessions | 4.2 | 5.2 | 5.0 | 3.7 | 9.0 | 43 |
| synthetic sessions + trend | 3.4 | 4.0 | 5.3 | 3.8 | 6.0 | 52 |
| shuffle + trend | 3.9 | 3.7 | 5.2 | 4.8 | 6.6 | 52 |
| local permutation | 3.7 | 4.5 | 5.0 | 3.9 | 5.0 | 52 |

Key observations:

- The uncorrected scan is the largest single error, 13.5% with no drift at all.
- Ignoring non-stationarity costs another factor of two to eight, but less than
  the action-value literature would suggest. The reason is that a binary
  outcome is mostly trial-to-trial noise around the learning curve, so only a
  small part of it is slow enough to line up with drift. In the real sessions
  the outcome residuals are even slightly anti-correlated at lag 1 (median
  -0.10): the animals tend to follow an error with a correct response.
- Four nulls are close to nominal with identical power. Simulation alone does
  not separate them. Synthetic sessions and cubic detrending start to slip
  (6-9%) when learning is step-like and the rate jumps, which is the case
  their assumptions do not cover.
- Run length for the local test hardly matters between 4 and 24 trials
  (`results/calibration_blocks.json`).

### What the real cells say

Same tests on the 22 public cells (`results/outcome_tests_summary.txt`):

| | previous outcome | next outcome (control) |
|---|---|---|
| per-window t, 3 in a row | 18 of 22 | 13 of 22 |
| shuffle | 12 | 6 |
| shuffle + trend | 11 | 4 |
| synthetic sessions + trend | 9 | 4 |
| session permutation + trend | 6 | 2 |
| local permutation | 10 | 2 |

With a correct test about one cell in 22 should pass the control. Thirteen is
not that (binomial p ~ 4e-12), six is not (p ~ 6e-4), four is doubtful
(p ~ .02), two is fine (p ~ .3). So on real data the detrending and
model-based nulls that looked acceptable in simulation leak, and the two that
make no assumption about the shape of the drift do not. Of those two, session
permutation has only 21 other sessions to draw on here, so its smallest
possible p is .045 and it finds fewer cells.

The model-based null had a second strike against it. Sessions simulated from
the fitted learning model do not look like the real ones: in 32% of real
sessions the observed late accuracy is outside the central 95% of the model's
simulations (monkeys end up more reliably correct than a smooth incremental
learner does). That is the IBL objection, shown on this task's own behaviour.

**Decision.** Local permutation is the primary test, the next-outcome control
is always reported beside it, and session permutation is the cross-check when
there are enough sessions.

With that test, 10 of 22 public cells carry the previous outcome (9 at a false
discovery rate of 5%), against 18 of 22 by the scan rule. Five fire more after
an error and five more after a reward, and for nine of the ten the window that
carries it falls 0.5-1.3 s before the next symbol, between trials and during
fixation. So the phenomenon is there and it has the reported shape.
What changes is how many cells one would put in the count.

Limits: 22 cells; my windows, not the authors'; my stand-in for the scan rule
(I had the preprint's description of delta epochs, not the full published
methods); and the label problem with the file. The local test also cannot
tell "outcome of the last trial" from anything else that changes for about one
trial after an error. The public file has no reaction times, licking or hand
kinematics to put in the model. The papers address those separately.

### Size of the signal across learning

I expected that choosing the epoch on all trials and then plotting the
difference by learning stage would manufacture a decline, because most error
trials are early and so early trials dominate the selection. Working it through
shows it does not: the covariance between a stage's difference and the pooled
difference is the same for every stage, so the selection bias is a constant
offset. Simulated cells with no signal confirm it: the selected-window
"effect" is about 0.5-0.75 sd at every stage, flat. The published decline is
therefore not something this bias could produce. What the bias does is make a
cell with nothing look like a cell with something, at all stages.

`tools/crossfit_timecourse.py` removes the offset: contrasts are taken within
runs of 8 trials (so drift cancels) and the window and sign applied to each
run are chosen from the other runs. Results (`docs/figures/learning_timecourse.png`):

| estimated p(correct) | < .6 | .6-.7 | .7-.8 | .8-.9 | > .9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| simulated, signal (72), selected on all trials | 1.50 | 1.14 | 0.82 | 0.59 | 0.57 |
| simulated, signal, cross-fitted | 1.23 | 0.99 | 0.58 | 0.39 | 0.27 |
| simulated, no signal (22), selected on all trials | 0.51 | 0.44 | 0.66 | 0.51 | 0.77 |
| simulated, no signal, cross-fitted | 0.00 | -0.39 | -0.16 | -0.13 | 0.11 |
| public (22), selected on all trials | 1.33 | 0.78 | 0.88 | 0.99 | 0.38 |
| public, cross-fitted | 0.64 | 0.68 | 0.58 | 0.57 | 0.39 |

In the public cells the cross-fitted signal is clearly above zero through
p = .9 (95% intervals exclude zero in the first four bins) and about half the
size the selected window suggests early on. It is lower in the last bin, in
the direction of the published decline, but with 22 cells that interval
(-0.28 to 1.24) is too wide to call.

## Other choices, briefly

**Learning stage.** Sessions are aligned on the state-space estimate of
p(correct) (Smith et al. 2004, J Neurosci 24:447; `tools/learning_curve.py`),
not on trial number or thirds of the block, because learning speed varies
several-fold between sessions. Stages for decoding use the lab's own split at
p = .75 (Ipata et al. 2024). The learning trial from the state-space model and
an 8-of-10 criterion agree for most sessions and disagree by tens of trials
for a few slow ones.

**Sorting.** Complex spikes are too rare (1-2% of events) for a mixture model
to find; the sorter fits the bulk robustly, takes what it cannot explain, and
keeps that group only if simple spikes pause after it, the P-sort logic
(Sedaghat-Nejad et al. 2021, J Neurophysiol 126:1055). Waveform stability of
0.85 between the start and end of a session is the lab's published criterion.
The other thresholds are mine.

**Encoding model.** A gradient-boosted model on the raw trial variables was
used as a yardstick for the GLM (Benjamin et al. 2018, Front Comput Neurosci
12:56). With a cubic trend for drift the trees explained three times the
held-out deviance of the GLM on the simulated cells. That gap was all drift. With a drift term whose
resolution is chosen per cell by cross-validation the GLM matches the trees on
simulated cells (0.136 vs 0.133) and beats them on 21 of 22 public cells
(0.170 vs 0.093). Held-out deviance is a blunt detector for a signal confined
to 250 ms of early-learning trials; the GLM is used for the shape and timing of
the kernel (true epoch time recovered with r = 0.70, median error 47 ms), not
for deciding which cells code.

**Decoding.** Pseudo-populations, linear classifier, per-cell train/test split
before pseudo-trials are drawn. Chance is estimated by relabelling recorded
trials within each cell and rebuilding everything. The shortcut of shuffling
labels among pseudo-trials gives a chance distribution four times too narrow
here (sd 0.029 against 0.124), because it counts 300 resampled pseudo-trials
as if they were 300 observations when each cell contributed a handful. For the
previous outcome, counts are centred within runs of trials before decoding;
without that, cells built with no outcome signal decode at 0.57 on average,
with it 0.50. Linear classifiers (LDA, logistic, SVM) are within a point of
each other; boosted trees are 14-23 points worse at these sample sizes.

In the public cells the previous outcome is decodable in early learning
(peak 0.92 against a 95% chance maximum of 0.89, one window of 25) and the
hand is decodable around movement at every stage (0.92-0.98).

**Complex spikes (simulation only).** Regressing complex-spike counts on
estimated p(error), previous outcome and current outcome recovers the built-in
dependence on p(error). It also assigns a small weight to the current trial's
outcome at symbol onset (median +0.10, p = .02), which cannot be real since
the outcome has not happened. It appears because p(error) is estimated with
error and the actual outcomes carry what the estimate missed. Worth
remembering before reading a small outcome coefficient in real complex-spike
data as outcome coding.
